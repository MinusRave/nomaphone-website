You just landed in Bangkok. It's 11 PM, the humidity hits you like a wall, and your phone says "No Service." Your Slack notifications are piling up. Your client in New York expects a call tomorrow morning. And you have no idea how to get online.

Welcome to the first 30 minutes of every digital nomad's Southeast Asia chapter.

The good news: staying connected in Southeast Asia as a nomad is surprisingly easy once you know the playbook. The region has solid mobile infrastructure, cheap data, and coworking spaces everywhere. The bad news: every country does it differently, and nobody tells you the details before you land.

This guide covers six countries — Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam, the Philippines, Cambodia, and Malaysia — with the specifics you actually need. SIM cards, data speeds, WiFi reliability, VoIP quirks, and what it really costs to call home.

## Thailand: The Easiest Starting Point

Thailand is where most Southeast Asia nomads begin, and for good reason. The connectivity infrastructure is excellent.

### SIM Cards and Carriers

Thailand has three major carriers: AIS, DTAC, and True Move. All three offer tourist SIM cards, and you can grab one at any 7-Eleven — which means you can be online within 10 minutes of leaving the airport.

**AIS** has the widest coverage, especially outside Bangkok. If you plan to visit islands or northern hill towns, AIS is your safest bet. A tourist SIM with 15 GB runs about 299 baht (roughly $9).

**True Move** is popular with nomads for competitive data packages. Similar pricing to AIS, excellent coverage in Bangkok and Chiang Mai.

**DTAC** merged with True in 2023, so coverage is the same network now. You might still see DTAC-branded SIMs, but they run on True's infrastructure.

**Pro tip:** Grab your SIM at the airport 7-Eleven or the carrier booth in arrivals. You need your passport. Five minutes and you're online.

### Data Speeds and WiFi

4G LTE is standard across Bangkok, Chiang Mai, and major tourist areas. Expect 20-50 Mbps download speeds on mobile data. 5G is available in Bangkok but coverage is spotty and you probably don't need it.

WiFi at coworking spaces in Thailand is generally reliable. Punspace in Chiang Mai, Hubba in Bangkok, and most established coworking spots deliver 50-100 Mbps. Coffee shop WiFi is hit-or-miss — some cafes offer solid connections, others give you 2 Mbps shared among 30 people.

### VoIP and Calling

Thailand doesn't block VoIP services. WhatsApp calls, FaceTime, Zoom, and browser-based calling all work fine. This matters because some countries in the region do restrict VoIP (more on that below).

Calling home from Thailand via carrier roaming is expensive. AT&T charges $2.00-$3.00 per minute. Even T-Mobile's "free" international roaming limits you to 2G speeds for data unless you pay extra.

## Indonesia: Bali and Beyond

Indonesia is the second most popular nomad destination in Southeast Asia, and Bali specifically has become a remote work hub. But connectivity here requires a bit more patience.

### SIM Cards and Carriers

**Telkomsel** is the dominant carrier with the best coverage across the archipelago. Their tourist SIM (called "simPATI Tourist") gives you data and some call minutes. You can buy one at the airport, but registration requires a passport and sometimes a local contact number.

**XL Axiata** is the budget alternative. Slightly cheaper data packages, slightly weaker coverage outside Java and Bali.

**Indosat (IM3)** is the third option. Coverage is decent in major cities but drops off fast in rural areas.

SIM registration in Indonesia got stricter in recent years. You need your passport number, and activation can sometimes take a few hours. Don't wait until you need it urgently.

### Data Speeds and WiFi

4G in Bali's main areas (Canggu, Seminyak, Ubud) is decent. Expect 10-30 Mbps. Outside tourist zones, speeds drop significantly. If you're heading to Nusa Penida or the Gili Islands, don't count on reliable mobile data.

WiFi in Bali coworking spaces is generally good. Dojo Bali, Outpost, and similar spots offer 30-80 Mbps. But Bali's general internet infrastructure has bad days. Power outages happen, undersea cables get damaged, and sometimes the whole island slows down for a day or two.

### VoIP and Calling

Indonesia doesn't officially block VoIP, but some services experience throttling during peak hours. WhatsApp calls usually work fine. Zoom and Google Meet are reliable in coworking spaces. Browser-based calling works without issues on a stable connection.

One thing to watch: Indonesian carriers sometimes throttle VPN traffic, which can affect VoIP quality if you're routing through a VPN.

## Vietnam: Fast Internet, Some Restrictions

Vietnam is a sleeper hit for digital nomads. It's affordable, the food is incredible, and the internet is actually faster than most people expect.

### SIM Cards and Carriers

**Viettel** is the largest carrier and has the best nationwide coverage. Tourist SIMs are available at the airport and phone shops. A data package with 30 GB costs around 200,000 VND (about $8).

**Mobifone** is the second-largest. Similar coverage in cities, slightly cheaper packages.

**Vinaphone** is the third option, fine for Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi but weaker in rural areas.

You can buy SIMs at the airport, but you'll often get a better deal at small phone shops in the city. Registration requires your passport.

### Data Speeds and WiFi

Vietnam's internet speeds are surprisingly good. 4G in Ho Chi Minh City and Hanoi regularly hits 30-50 Mbps. WiFi at cafes and coworking spaces is often faster than what you'd find in many European cities.

Coworking spaces like Dreamplex in HCMC and Toong in Hanoi offer reliable 50-100 Mbps connections.

### VoIP and Calling

Here's where Vietnam gets tricky. The government has been known to block or throttle certain VoIP services periodically. Facebook Messenger calls and FaceTime have experienced disruptions in the past.

WhatsApp calling generally works, but quality can be inconsistent. Browser-based calling (like NomaPhone) typically works because it uses standard web protocols that are harder to selectively block.

If you rely on voice calls for work, have a backup plan. A local SIM with a decent data package plus browser-based calling gives you the most reliable setup.

## The Philippines: Great Coverage, Variable Quality

The Philippines has improved its mobile infrastructure significantly, though it still has more variability than Thailand or Vietnam.

### SIM Cards and Carriers

**Globe** and **Smart** are the two major carriers, and they're roughly equal in coverage and quality.

Globe is slightly better in Metro Manila and tourist areas. Smart has better rural coverage in some regions. Both offer prepaid tourist SIMs for around 500 PHP ($9) with decent data allowances.

You can buy SIMs at airports, malls, and the small "sari-sari" stores that are everywhere. Registration requires a valid ID (passport works).

### Data Speeds and WiFi

4G speeds in Manila average 15-30 Mbps. In Cebu and popular nomad spots like Siargao or Dumaguete, expect 10-20 Mbps on a good day. The Philippines has historically had some of the slowest internet speeds in Southeast Asia, though it's improved significantly since 2024.

Coworking WiFi is your best bet for reliable work. KMC in Makati, ASPACE in BGC, and Clock In in Cebu all offer solid connections. Coffee shop WiFi is unreliable — plan your video calls around coworking access.

### VoIP and Calling

No VoIP restrictions in the Philippines. All major calling services work fine. The challenge here is bandwidth, not blocking. If your connection is slow, voice quality suffers. Use a wired connection or strong WiFi for important calls.

## Cambodia: Surprisingly Good Mobile Data

Cambodia catches a lot of nomads off guard. The mobile internet is actually quite good, even though the country's overall infrastructure is still developing.

### SIM Cards and Carriers

**Cellcard** is the most popular carrier among foreigners. Good coverage in Phnom Penh, Siem Reap, and Sihanoukville. Tourist SIMs are cheap — about $2-5 for a SIM with several gigabytes of data.

**Smart (Cambodia)** — not related to Smart Philippines — is the second option. Comparable coverage in major cities.

**Metfone** is the budget option with decent urban coverage.

You can buy SIMs at the airport, phone shops, and even some guesthouses. Registration is quick and just needs your passport.

### Data Speeds and WiFi

4G in Phnom Penh delivers 10-25 Mbps. Siem Reap is slightly slower. Outside major cities, coverage gets patchy fast.

Coworking spaces in Phnom Penh — like TriSpace and The Desk — offer decent WiFi at 20-50 Mbps. Cambodia's overall internet infrastructure relies heavily on connections through Thailand and Vietnam, so speeds can fluctuate.

### VoIP and Calling

No VoIP restrictions. Cambodia is one of the more open countries in the region when it comes to internet freedom. All calling methods work without issues.

## Malaysia: Reliable and Modern

Malaysia has some of the best digital infrastructure in Southeast Asia. Kuala Lumpur in particular is an underrated nomad hub.

### SIM Cards and Carriers

**Hotlink** (by Maxis) is the go-to for tourists and nomads. Excellent coverage nationwide and straightforward tourist packages. A prepaid SIM with 15 GB of data costs about 35 MYR ($8).

**Digi** is the budget alternative with good urban coverage. Slightly weaker in rural Borneo.

**Celcom** rounds out the big three. Strong coverage, competitive pricing.

You can buy SIMs at the airport (KLIA has carrier booths in arrivals), convenience stores, and phone shops. Passport required for registration.

### Data Speeds and WiFi

Malaysia's 4G network is fast. Expect 30-60 Mbps in KL, Penang, and Johor Bahru. 5G is rolling out in KL and delivers impressive speeds where available.

Coworking WiFi is excellent. WORQ, Common Ground, and Colony in KL all offer 100+ Mbps connections. Even mall WiFi in Malaysia tends to be faster than cafe WiFi in most other SEA countries.

### VoIP and Calling

No restrictions. Malaysia is completely open for VoIP services. WhatsApp, Zoom, FaceTime, and browser-based calling all work perfectly. This, combined with the fast internet, makes Malaysia one of the most hassle-free countries for staying connected.

## Quick Tips for Staying Connected Across SEA

A few practical things that apply everywhere in the region:

**Carry two SIMs or use eSIM.** If your phone supports eSIM, keep your home carrier on eSIM (for receiving SMS and 2FA codes) and use a local SIM for data. This is the single most useful connectivity hack for nomads.

**Download offline maps before you arrive.** Google Maps offline mode saves you from burning data on navigation when you're in a taxi from the airport.

**Test your calling setup before you need it.** Don't wait until your bank freezes your card to figure out how international calling works. Make a test call to a friend or family member on your first day.

**Know the local emergency numbers.** Thailand: 191 (police), 1669 (ambulance). Indonesia: 112. Vietnam: 113 (police), 115 (ambulance). Philippines: 911. Cambodia: 117 (police), 119 (ambulance). Malaysia: 999.

## A Note on VPNs

Many nomads run a VPN for security or to access home-country services. One thing to know: VPNs can slow your connection by 20-40%, which affects call quality. If you're on a VoIP call, disconnect your VPN for the duration unless you specifically need it.

Vietnam and Cambodia occasionally throttle VPN traffic. WireGuard tends to perform better than OpenVPN in the region. Browser-based calling services work without a VPN in every Southeast Asian country.

## The Bottom Line

Southeast Asia is one of the most nomad-friendly regions on the planet for connectivity. Local SIMs are cheap, data is plentiful, coworking spaces are everywhere, and VoIP restrictions are minimal (with Vietnam being the notable exception).

The playbook is simple: grab a local SIM when you land, find a coworking space with solid WiFi, and set up a browser-based calling solution for reaching real phone numbers back home. The infrastructure supports it.
