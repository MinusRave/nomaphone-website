The first year we went nomad, our phone bill was embarrassing.

We're talking $50 to $80 a month just to save money on international calls as a nomad — and failing at it. Roaming charges from random countries. Skype credits that expired. Google Voice sessions that dropped mid-call because the Wi-Fi in Chiang Mai had other plans.

One month it hit $92. For phone calls. In a country where rent was $400.

That was the wake-up call (pun intended). Something had to change.

Fast forward to today, and our total calling costs sit under $10 a month. Every month. Regardless of which country we're in. That's a savings of roughly $500 to $900 per year, depending on how chatty you are.

Here's exactly how we got there — the mistakes, the math, and the setup that finally worked.

## The "Before" Phase: When Every Call Cost a Small Fortune

If you've just started the nomad life, this part will sound painfully familiar.

### Carrier Roaming: The Silent Budget Killer

When we first left the US, we kept our T-Mobile plan active. Seemed like the safe move. T-Mobile offers free international roaming in 200+ countries, right?

Sort of. The "free" part covers data (at 2G speeds) and texting. Actual voice calls? Those run $0.25 per minute on the Magenta plan. That doesn't sound terrible until you're on hold with your bank for 45 minutes.

A single 45-minute call to Chase at $0.25/min = $11.25. Do that twice a month and you're already at $22.50 just for bank calls.

And that's T-Mobile — the "international-friendly" carrier. AT&T charges $2.00 to $3.00 per minute for international roaming. Verizon hits you with $2.99 per minute or pushes you toward their $10/day TravelPass.

One team member kept their AT&T plan for three months. Their average monthly phone bill during that stretch: $78. For maybe 30 minutes of actual calls.

### The Skype Era (and Why It Died)

Like every other nomad, we tried Skype. For years it was the default answer to "how do I call a US number from abroad?"

Skype worked — kind of. The rates were reasonable. But the experience was rough:

- Call quality was inconsistent, especially on slower connections
- You needed the app installed on every device
- Credits expired if you didn't use them within a certain window
- The interface got worse with every update

Then Microsoft shut Skype down entirely in May 2025. Millions of people who relied on it for international calls suddenly needed an alternative. If you're curious about what replaced it, we've written about that separately.

### Google Voice: Free but Frustrating

Google Voice was our next attempt. Free calls to US numbers? Sign us up.

The problems showed up fast:

- **US-only signup.** You need a US phone number to verify your account. Once you're abroad full-time without a US SIM, creating a new account is impossible.
- **Quality issues from Asia.** Calls from Southeast Asia had consistent lag and echo. Bearable for catching up with friends, terrible for a bank call where you need to hear every digit of a confirmation code.
- **VPN dependency.** Some Google Voice features get spotty or blocked outside the US. We found ourselves routing through VPNs just to make it work, which added another layer of latency.
- **No real phone number portability.** You can port a US number into Google Voice, but the reverse is painful if you ever want to leave.

Google Voice is a solid tool if you're US-based and making occasional international calls. For full-time nomads calling from Lisbon or Bali or Medellin, it's a constant headache.

## The Math That Changed Everything

We sat down one afternoon in a coworking space in Lisbon and did the actual math. Not the "I think I spend about..." math. The real, look-at-your-bank-statements math.

Here's what a typical nomad's calling pattern looks like:

- 2-3 bank/financial calls per month (average 30 minutes each, including hold time)
- 3-4 business calls per month (average 15 minutes each)
- 2-3 personal calls per month to US landlines or mobiles (average 20 minutes each)
- Occasional government or utility calls (1-2 per month, average 20 minutes)

**Total: roughly 250-350 minutes per month of outbound calls to US/international numbers.**

## The Switch: Browser-Based VoIP Plus Local SIM

The solution turned out to be embarrassingly simple. Two things:

1. **A local SIM card for data** (wherever you happen to be)
2. **A browser-based VoIP service for all outbound calls**

That's it. No apps to install. No complicated forwarding setups. No VPNs.

### Why Browser-Based Calling

Browser-based VoIP means you open a website, dial a number, and call. It works on your laptop, your tablet, your phone — anything with a browser and an internet connection.

The key advantages over app-based solutions:

- **No downloads.** You're not installing and updating yet another app across all your devices.
- **Works on any operating system.** Mac, Windows, Linux, ChromeOS, Android, iOS. If it has a browser, it makes calls.
- **No account lock-in drama.** Most browser VoIP services are pay-as-you-go. Buy credits, use them, done.
- **Consistent quality.** Modern browser calling uses the same underlying technology (WebRTC) that powers Google Meet and Zoom. If your internet can handle a video call, it can handle a voice call.

### How We Set It Up

**Step 1: Cancel or pause the US carrier plan.**

This was the scary part. Letting go of the T-Mobile plan felt like cutting a safety net. But we ported our US number to Google Voice (which costs a one-time $20 fee) so we could still receive texts and SMS verification codes.

Monthly savings: $50-70.

**Step 2: Buy local SIM cards for data.**

In most countries, a prepaid data SIM costs between $5 and $20 per month for plenty of data. In Thailand, you can get 30GB for about $10. In Portugal, 10GB runs around $12. In Mexico, 6GB is roughly $8.

We only need the SIM for data. All voice calls go through the browser.

Monthly cost: $5-20, depending on the country.

**Step 3: Use browser-based VoIP for all outbound calls.**

This is where the real savings happen. Browser VoIP rates to the US typically range from $0.005 to $0.03 per minute, depending on the provider.

Let's use a realistic middle-ground rate of $0.03 per minute (which is what NomaPhone charges for US/Canada calls). At 300 minutes per month:

300 minutes x $0.03 = **$9.00 per month**

Compare that to $75/month on T-Mobile roaming or $600+/month on AT&T roaming.

## Choosing the Right Browser VoIP Service

Not all browser-based calling services are equal. Here's what to look for:

### Pay-As-You-Go vs. Subscriptions

Go with pay-as-you-go. Subscriptions make sense if you're calling the same amount every month. Nomads don't. Some months you're on the phone constantly (tax season, visa paperwork). Other months you barely make a call.

Pay-as-you-go means you only spend what you use. Look for services where credits never expire — that way you're not losing money during quiet months.

### Call Quality for Important Calls

This matters more than price. A dropped call with your bank while they're verifying your identity is worse than paying an extra penny per minute.

The cheapest VoIP options (some as low as $0.005/min) sometimes cut corners on call quality. Audio compression gets aggressive. Connections drop. Hold music turns into static.

For calls that matter — banks, government offices, business clients — you want carrier-grade reliability. That might cost $0.03/min instead of $0.005/min. The difference on a 30-minute call is $0.75 versus $0.15. Both are negligible compared to roaming rates.

NomaPhone charges $0.03/min to the US and focuses specifically on call quality and reliability. YadaPhone offers $0.02/min. DialAnyone goes as low as $0.005/min. All are browser-based. The right choice depends on whether you prioritize rock-bottom cost or consistent quality for important calls.

### SMS and 2FA Support

This is the hidden gotcha. Many VoIP services let you make calls but can't receive SMS messages — especially the short-code verification texts that banks send.

If you need to receive 2FA codes via SMS (and you probably do), check whether your VoIP provider supports it before committing. Not all of them do.

### Country Coverage

Make sure the service covers the countries you actually call. US/Canada coverage is standard. But if you regularly call UK mobiles, Indian landlines, or Australian numbers, check the rates for those destinations specifically.

UK mobile rates, for example, can vary widely — from $0.05/min to $0.20/min depending on the provider.

## Tips for Other Nomads Looking to Cut Phone Costs

Here's what we'd tell our past selves:

### 1. Port Your US Number Before You Leave

If you have a US phone number you want to keep, port it to Google Voice or a virtual number service before you cancel your carrier plan. Once you're abroad without a US SIM, porting becomes much harder.

The Google Voice port costs $20 one time. It lets you keep your number for receiving texts and 2FA codes indefinitely.

### 2. Buy Local SIMs at the Airport

In most countries, you can grab a prepaid data SIM right at the airport arrivals hall. Don't overthink this. Get the cheapest data-only plan that covers your needs (usually 5-15GB per month) and move on.

You'll replace it when you move to the next country anyway.

### 3. Test Your VoIP Setup Before You Need It

Don't wait until your bank locks your card to figure out browser-based calling. Set it up on a calm Tuesday afternoon. Make a test call. Make sure audio works in both directions. Confirm you can dial the specific numbers you need (toll-free numbers, international numbers, etc.).

### 4. Keep a Backup Calling Method

Wi-Fi goes down. Browsers crash. Always have a plan B. For us, that's a secondary VoIP service with a few dollars of credits loaded. The chance of both your primary and backup failing simultaneously is near zero.

### 5. Track Your Actual Spending

Most browser VoIP services show your call history and costs. Check it monthly for the first few months. You might discover you're spending even less than expected — or that certain call types (like UK mobile calls) cost more than you assumed.

### 6. Don't Overlook Wi-Fi Calling Quality

Browser-based calling is only as good as your internet connection. In a coworking space with solid Wi-Fi, call quality is excellent. In a beach shack with satellite internet, maybe not so much.

For important calls, find a spot with reliable Wi-Fi first. Most coworking spaces and hotel lobbies work great. Coffee shops with lots of patrons streaming video? Less reliable.

## What About WhatsApp and FaceTime?

We get this question a lot. "Why not just use WhatsApp or FaceTime for everything?"

Two reasons:

**First, the other person needs the app too.** You can WhatsApp your friends. You cannot WhatsApp your bank's fraud department. You cannot FaceTime the IRS. You cannot send a voice message to the insurance company's hold queue.

Anytime you need to call a landline, a business, a government office, or anyone who isn't already in your contacts with the same app — you need actual phone calling capability.

**Second, those apps are caller-to-caller only.** They don't dial real phone numbers. Browser-based VoIP actually connects to the phone network. You dial a number, it rings on the other end, exactly like a regular phone call. Because it is a regular phone call — just routed over the internet instead of through a cell tower.

WhatsApp and FaceTime are great for staying in touch with people who use them. They don't replace a phone.

## The Bigger Picture: Why This Matters for Nomad Life

Saving $500 a year on phone calls isn't going to make you rich. But it's part of a larger pattern that separates sustainable nomads from people who burn through their savings in 18 months.

The nomad lifestyle works because of cost arbitrage. You earn in a strong currency and spend in places where costs are lower. But that only works if you're actually paying attention to where money leaks out.

Phone costs are one of those leaks. International banking fees are another. Currency conversion markups. Foreign ATM charges. Unnecessary subscriptions you forgot to cancel.

Individually, each one is "only" $20 to $80 a month. Combined, they can eat through $2,000 to $5,000 a year without you noticing.

The phone bill was our first fix. It was also the easiest. One afternoon of setup. No ongoing maintenance. The savings just happen automatically, month after month.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I keep my US phone number if I cancel my carrier plan?

Yes. Port it to Google Voice ($20 one-time fee) before canceling. You'll keep the number for texts and some calls. For outbound calls to US numbers, use a browser-based VoIP service.

### Does browser-based calling work in countries with restricted internet?

In most countries, yes. Browser calling uses the same protocols as video conferencing, which works almost everywhere. In countries with heavy internet restrictions (like China), you may need a VPN — but that's true for most internet services in those locations.

### What if I need to receive incoming calls on a US number?

You have a few options. Google Voice can forward calls to a VoIP number. Some browser VoIP services offer virtual US numbers that can receive incoming calls. NomaPhone, for example, provides US virtual numbers with SMS/2FA support.

### Is the call quality actually good enough for business calls?

On a decent internet connection (5+ Mbps), browser-based calling sounds indistinguishable from a regular phone call. We've done client calls, bank calls, and even podcast recordings over browser VoIP without issues. The key variable is your internet connection, not the calling technology.

### How do I handle calls if my internet is down?

This is the one genuine limitation. If you have zero internet access, browser-based calling won't work. In practice, this rarely happens — most nomads are never far from Wi-Fi or mobile data. But for true emergencies, having a local SIM with some voice minutes is a reasonable backup.
