You live in Thailand but your US clients expect a US number. Your landlord in Lisbon wants a Portuguese number to call. Your parents won't dial an international number no matter how many times you explain it.

A virtual phone number can solve problems like these. It's a real phone number, in a country you choose, that rings in an app or on your laptop wherever you are. But virtual numbers also have real limits, especially for bank verification codes, and plenty of people buy one when what they actually need is a cheap way to make calls.

This guide explains how virtual numbers work, the main types, what providers typically charge, the rules many countries apply, why banks often reject them, and how to choose a provider. One disclosure up front: NomaPhone does not sell virtual numbers. We cover the topic because readers ask about it constantly, and because the right answer is sometimes "you don't need one."

## What Are Virtual Phone Numbers

A virtual phone number is a real telephone number that isn't tied to a SIM card or a physical line. Calls to it reach the provider's servers and are delivered to you over the internet (in an app, a browser or a desk phone) or forwarded to another number.

**Also called:** VoIP numbers, cloud numbers, online numbers, DID (Direct Inward Dialing) numbers, second-line numbers.

### How they differ from a regular mobile number

| | Mobile number (SIM) | Virtual number |
|---|---|---|
| Tied to | A SIM card and a carrier | An account with a provider |
| Abroad | Roaming charges or no service | Works anywhere with internet |
| Incoming calls | Ring your phone | Ring an app, or forward elsewhere |
| SMS | Everything, including short codes | Often limited (see below) |
| Bank 2FA | Usually accepted | Often rejected |
| Emergency calls | Yes | Usually not, or limited |

## How Virtual Numbers Work

### Receiving calls

1. Someone dials your number, for example a US +1 number.
2. The call travels through the normal phone network to your provider.
3. The provider converts it to an internet call and delivers it to your app or browser, or forwards it to another number.

The caller pays whatever they normally pay to call that country. If your virtual number is local to them, the call is local for them, even if you're on the other side of the world.

### Making calls

Most virtual number apps also let you call out and show your virtual number as caller ID, so people can call you back on it. Outgoing calls are usually billed per minute on top of the monthly fee.

### SMS

Many virtual numbers can send and receive regular texts, but support varies by country and number type. Common gaps: messages from short codes (the 5-6 digit senders banks and apps use), MMS, and verification texts from services that filter out VoIP numbers. Business texting in the US also requires registration (A2P 10DLC) with most providers.

## Types of Virtual Numbers

### Local (geographic) numbers

Tied to a city or area code, such as a 415 number in San Francisco or an 020 number in London. Best when you want to look local to a specific place, or want family in one city to call you at local rates. Usually the cheapest and most widely available type.

### Mobile numbers

Look like a mobile number in that country (UK 07, most European mobile ranges). Some services and some callers prefer mobile numbers, and in countries where mobile and landline numbers look different, a mobile number is often the only kind that can receive SMS. Availability is much more limited than local numbers, and requirements are stricter.

### Toll-free numbers

Free for the caller, while the owner pays per minute: US and Canada 800/888/877/866/855/844/833, UK 0800, Australia 1800. Good for customer support lines. Expect a higher monthly fee plus per-minute charges for every incoming call. A toll-free number usually can't receive texts unless the provider specifically enables it.

### National (non-geographic) numbers

Numbers that aren't tied to a city, such as UK 03 numbers. Common for businesses serving a whole country.

### Virtual phone number in a specific country: what to expect

The biggest factor usually isn't price, it's **regulation**. Many countries treat phone numbers as a national resource and require providers to know who holds each one:

- **US and Canada:** generally the easiest. Most providers only ask for an email and a payment card, although some free services require an existing US number to sign up.
- **UK:** local numbers are easy to get; providers increasingly ask for a name and address.
- **EU countries:** geographic numbers commonly require proof of ID and an address in the country, sometimes in the same area code region (Germany is a well-known example). Mobile-range virtual numbers are rarer.
- **Australia:** local and mobile numbers often require an Australian address.
- **India, China, the Gulf states and others:** heavily restricted. Be wary of anyone selling these cheaply without any verification.

If a provider asks you to upload an ID or proof of address, that's normal compliance, not a scam. If a provider offers a number in a tightly regulated country with no checks at all, that number may be withdrawn without notice.

## Common Use Cases

### Staying reachable for people in one country

Family, clients or tenants in one country call a local number, and it rings in your app wherever you are. This is the classic, and strongest, use case.

### Business presence

A consultant in Portugal with UK clients gets a London number, publishes it, and answers calls in an app. Many business phone providers bundle this with voicemail, call routing and team features.

### Keeping a "home" number while abroad

Some people port their old mobile number to a virtual provider so they don't lose it. This can work, but read the limitations below before relying on it for banking. See also our guide on [keeping your US number while living abroad](/blog/how-to-keep-us-number-while-living-abroad/).

### Digital nomads: one stable number across borders

Picking up a local SIM in each new country is great for data, but it's a liability if your accounts are tied to whatever number you had last. Every time you retire a SIM you risk losing:

- **Bank and payment access**, when login or transaction approvals rely on SMS codes.
- **Government and tax portals** back home, which still send codes to the number on file.
- **2FA on everyday services** like Google, Microsoft and PayPal, where changing the number mid-trip can turn into a long account recovery.
- **Client and employer contact**, because handing out a new number every few weeks causes missed calls.

Keeping your home plan on roaming is the usual fallback, but carrier roaming can cost several dollars a minute for calls, on top of the monthly plan you're paying just so the number doesn't get recycled.

A setup that works for many long-term travelers:

1. **Keep your original number alive cheaply** where possible: move it to a low-cost carrier (in the US, Tello or US Mobile, which can receive texts over Wi-Fi calling), an eSIM plan, or a number-parking service. This is usually the most robust option for bank 2FA.
2. **Set up a virtual number before you leave** if you want one. Google Voice, for example, needs an existing US number to sign up.
3. **Move accounts to authenticator apps or push approvals** wherever you can, so they don't depend on a phone number at all.
4. **Test every important account** with the number you plan to use before you go.

This matters most for long-term travelers who move every few weeks or months, expats with financial and legal ties to their home country, and remote workers or freelancers who need to stay reachable for clients in a specific country.

## Limitations and Drawbacks

### Banks and many apps reject virtual numbers for 2FA

This is the limitation that catches most people. Banks, payment apps and many online services look up the "line type" of the number you register. Numbers classified as VoIP, especially "non-fixed VoIP" numbers anyone can sign up for online, are frequently refused for SMS verification or never receive the codes. The reasons:

- **Fraud prevention:** online numbers are cheap and easy to create in bulk, so they show up often in account-takeover and fake-account fraud.
- **Identity checks:** a carrier mobile number is linked to a contract or a registered SIM; a virtual number often isn't.
- **Short codes:** many banks send codes from short codes that VoIP numbers can't receive.

Some services accept certain virtual numbers (Google Voice is accepted by some US services and rejected by others). There's no reliable list, and policies change. If 2FA is your main reason for wanting a number, more robust options are an authenticator app, passkeys or in-app approval if your bank supports them, or keeping a real mobile line active on a cheap plan. Our guides on [2FA codes abroad](/blog/international-sms-2fa-codes-abroad/) and [whether 2FA works on a foreign SIM](/blog/will-2fa-work-foreign-sim/) go deeper.

If a code doesn't arrive, you can usually still call the bank and verify by voice, a call you can make from the browser with NomaPhone.

### Emergency calls usually don't work

Most virtual numbers can't reliably reach 911, 112 or 999, or can't pass your location to responders. Always keep a local SIM where you actually are.

### Quality depends on your internet

Calls in and out run over your connection. Weak café Wi-Fi or patchy mobile data means choppy audio, whichever provider you use.

### You don't fully control the number

If your account lapses or the provider shuts down, you can lose the number. Porting out of a virtual provider is possible in some countries and not in others, and free numbers are often reclaimed after a period of inactivity. Skype numbers, for example, ended along with Skype in 2025.

### Caller ID and spam filtering

Calls from virtual numbers are more likely to be labeled "spam likely" or blocked by some corporate phone systems.

## Setting Up a Virtual Number

### Types of providers

- **Free or ad-supported US numbers:** Google Voice (needs an existing US number to sign up and is aimed at US users), TextNow.
- **Second-line apps:** Hushed, Burner and similar, with numbers in a handful of countries.
- **Business phone systems:** OpenPhone, Dialpad, RingCentral, Grasshopper and others, offering numbers plus voicemail, routing and team features.
- **Developer platforms:** Twilio, Telnyx, Vonage. Often the cheapest per number, but you build the call handling yourself.
- **Number parking and cheap mobile plans:** services like NumberBarn park a number for a small monthly fee, and low-cost US carriers like Tello or US Mobile can keep a real mobile number alive cheaply.

We don't endorse any one of these. Prices and features change, so compare current offers.

### How to choose a provider

1. **Start from the job.** Incoming calls only? Texts? 2FA? Outgoing calls? Team use?
2. **Check that the country and number type you need** are available, and what documents are required.
3. **Read the SMS fine print.** Does it receive short codes? Does it work with the specific services you care about?
4. **Add up the total cost:** monthly fee, setup fee, per-minute charges in and out, SMS.
5. **Check porting.** Can you take the number with you if you leave?
6. **Check inactivity rules.** Free numbers are often reclaimed if unused.

### Test before you depend on it

Call it from another phone, send it a text, try it with the bank or app you need, and make an outgoing call. Don't switch your bank's 2FA to a new number until you've confirmed codes arrive.

## Virtual Numbers vs Real SIM Cards

**Use a real local SIM (or eSIM) when you need:** emergency calls, reliable mobile data, SMS from any sender, bank and government verification, a number officially registered to you.

**Use a virtual number when you need:** a presence in a country you don't live in, several countries' numbers on one device, people to reach you at local rates, a separate work line.

**Use neither when you need:** to place a call to a number abroad. A browser calling service or a calling app covers that without owning any number.

Many long-term travelers combine all three: a local SIM where they are, a cheap plan or virtual number to stay reachable at home, and pay-per-call for the occasional bank or government call.

## Tax and Legal Considerations

A virtual number doesn't make you resident anywhere, isn't an address and doesn't affect your tax or visa status. It's also not anonymous: providers keep records, and in many countries they're legally required to verify who holds each number. Using a number to pose as a local business or to get around KYC rules can break provider terms and local law.

## FAQ

**Can I get a virtual phone number in any country?**
No. Availability depends on local regulation. US, Canada and UK numbers are easy; many European numbers need ID and a local address; some countries are effectively off-limits to foreign providers.

**Will a virtual number work for my bank's verification codes?**
Possibly, but don't count on it. Many banks reject VoIP numbers or can't deliver codes to them. Test first, and set up an authenticator app or another backup.
